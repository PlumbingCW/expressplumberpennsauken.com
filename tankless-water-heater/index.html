Does a tankless unit suit your house?

Tankless sizing is not about gallons. It is about how much water must be heated per minute and by how many degrees. Add up the fixtures likely to run at once, then compare to the flow rate the unit can deliver at your incoming water temperature.

Cold groundwater needs a bigger temperature rise than warm water, so the same unit performs differently in January. A plumber calculates this on site instead of trusting a brochure number.

Flushing and descaling a tankless heater

Minerals in water settle out when it is heated. In a tankless unit, they coat the heat exchanger and narrow its small passages. The result is lower flow, hotter-than-set spikes, noise and eventually error codes.

A flush circulates a descaling solution through the unit and then rinses it. Service valves on the water lines make that possible, so a unit installed without them needs valves added first.

How often is a manufacturer question. Follow the manufacturer's schedule in your manual, because it differs by brand, model and water quality. If you cannot find the manual, give the dispatcher the model number and we can look up the requirements.

Worth knowing about local water

Do not assume your water is soft or hard. Ask the Merchantville Pennsauken Water Commission or New Jersey American Water for the latest annual water quality report, and test a sample from your own tap. Scale can change from street to street, so the report only tells part of the story.

Error codes, beeping and lukewarm water

Tankless units report faults through codes, and a display or beep tells you something is off. The meaning of a code changes by brand, so look it up in the manual, then tell the dispatcher the number. Do not open the cabinet or bypass safety parts yourself.

Ignition or flame faults

Often a gas supply, a dirty sensor or a failed igniter.

Exhaust and venting faults

A blocked or damaged vent trips the unit for safety.

Flow and scale faults

Low flow or overheating often traces back to scale or a clogged filter screen.

Leaks

Water from the cabinet or a connection means shut off the water and power and call.

Installing or retrofitting a tankless unit

A retrofit replaces a tank with a wall-hung unit, so the gas line, vent and pipework change. A new install in a remodel can place the unit where it vents most easily. Either way, the plumber checks the gas line size, the vent path and the condensate route before you order.

If you are weighing tankless against a tank, see our water heater installation planning page, and if your old tank has failed, water heater replacement explains that decision.

Condensing gas models also drain acidic condensate, which needs a neutralizer or a safe drain path. Electric models skip the venting but demand a heavy circuit, so a full panel can rule them out. Houses in Pennsauken, NJ with 1960s-era panels deserve a capacity check first.

Standards and rules behind this advice

Gas tankless units are listed to ANSI Z21.10.3, and the manufacturer's installation instructions set the venting, gas supply and clearance requirements. Department of Energy sizing guidelines work from flow rate and temperature rise, which is the math a plumber should show you.
