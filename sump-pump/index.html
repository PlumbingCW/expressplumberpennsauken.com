When a stopped sump pump becomes an emergency

A pump that quits during a storm gives you very little time. Once the pit overflows, water spreads across the slab and reaches the furnace, the water heater and anything stored on the floor. We serve Collingswood basements with the same approach.

Do not wade into water if the panel, outlets or extension cords are underwater or wet. Move valuables up, and call for an emergency visit the moment the pit stops draining. Night and weekend storms are exactly when the line is busiest, so call early.

If water is also coming through the floor drain, the problem may be the sewer side rather than the pump. Our basement waterproofing page explains how the pieces fit together.

Pump will not start, will not stop, or never rests

Will not start

Check that the power cord is plugged into a working outlet and that its GFCI has not tripped. Then lift the float by hand. If the pump stays quiet, the motor or switch has failed.

Will not stop

A float stuck against the pit wall or a failed switch keeps the motor running. A running pump with no water can burn out in a short time, so unplug it if you cannot free the float.

Runs constantly

If it empties the pit and refills right away, water may be flowing back from the discharge pipe through a bad check valve. Or the water table may simply be high after heavy rain.

Runs but moves little

A humming motor with weak output often means a clogged intake or a worn impeller. A frozen or blocked discharge line produces the same result.

Float switch, check valve and discharge line

Most sump trouble starts with one of three parts, not the motor itself. Knowing which one is failing saves money, because a switch or valve costs far less than a new pump.

Backup pumps: battery and water-powered types

A second pump covers two risks: the primary failing and the power going out. Battery backups run on a stored charge and need a charger and a replacement cycle. Water-powered backups use pressure from the water supply and need no electricity, but they draw on the supply while running.

Whether a water-powered unit is allowed at your address is a question for your water provider and the Construction Code Department. A battery backup with an alarm is the more common choice for homes.

Sewage ejector pumps for basement bathrooms

A bathroom or laundry below the level of the sewer line cannot drain by gravity. A sealed ejector pit collects waste and pumps it up to the house drain. It is not the same as a sump pit, and it must never be connected to a storm discharge.

If an ejector alarm sounds, stop using the toilet and sink in that room. Avoid contact with any sewage on the floor and call for service.

The Camden County Municipal Utilities Authority is funding a project in Pennsauken to separate combined stormwater and sanitary sewers. The stated aim is less localized flooding and better water quality. It does not change what your own basement needs, so keep the pump working.

Pre-storm sump check

Ten minutes before heavy rain can prevent a flooded basement.

  • Pour a bucket of water into the pit and watch the pump start and stop.
  • Confirm the discharge outlet outside is clear and the water flows away from the house.
  • Check that the cord is plugged into a GFCI outlet that resets.
  • Test the battery backup alarm and look at the battery date.
  • Lift stored boxes off the floor.
  • Keep the 24/7 number where you can find it in the dark.

Standards and rules behind this advice

The EPA and Ready.gov both advise that a sump pump with a battery backup is a sensible flood preparation guideline. Wiring for the pump circuit falls under the National Electrical Code (NEC), and discharge connections to the sanitary sewer need approvals, so ask the Construction Code Department first.
