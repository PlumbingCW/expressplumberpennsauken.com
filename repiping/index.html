Repiping in Pennsauken, NJ

Brown water, weak pressure and repeat pinhole leaks usually mean the pipes themselves are wearing out, not just one fixture. Repiping in Pennsauken, NJ replaces the supply plumbing in your house with new pipe, either all at once or in planned phases.

Signs your pipes may be due for replacement

Brown or yellow water

Rusty water that clears slowly after running often means steel pipe is shedding scale inside. A water heater problem can look similar, so we check both.

Pressure that fades over time

Interior buildup narrows older steel pipe, so flow weakens at fixtures far from the main. Showers upstairs usually suffer first.

Pinhole leaks in more than one place

A single leak is a repair. A second and third pinhole in the same system, often in different rooms, suggest the whole run is thin.

Stains or damp spots near pipes

Green crust on copper, rust trails on steel and soft drywall around a pipe all point to slow leaks.

Water tests or a lead line concern

If testing or the utility's records raise a flag about lead, replacing the suspect pipe belongs on the planning list.

None of these alone proves you need a repipe. A plumber should inspect the pipe, test pressure, and check how the system is behaving before suggesting it.

What a 1964 house usually has behind the walls

Pennsauken's median home was built in 1964, with older homes concentrated in the south and newer construction toward the northeast. Homes of that era often have copper supply lines, and some earlier ones still carry galvanized steel. Repairs and remodels over the decades also mean a single house may hold two or three different pipe materials.

That mix is why we inspect before quoting. A house with a recent copper addition and galvanized behind the original kitchen needs a different plan from one that is galvanized throughout. We do not state how long a given pipe should last, since water conditions and installation quality change the answer.

PEX or copper: an honest comparison

Both are accepted choices for home supply plumbing, and neither is right for every house. The decision comes down to access, budget, water conditions and what you prefer to live with.

Walls, access and how much gets opened

The cost and mess of repiping come mostly from reaching the pipe, not from the pipe itself. In a house with an open basement, most of the supply plumbing runs along joists and can be replaced with little demolition. Risers inside walls to upstairs bathrooms and kitchens are the hard part.

Small access holes, often placed in closets or behind fixtures, let a plumber fish new pipe through existing cavities. Patching the holes is separate work, and we tell you up front what is included and what you will want a drywall or paint contractor to handle.

Whole-house repipe or phased replacement?

A phased approach suits you if

  • Only part of the house has bad pipe, such as one bathroom group.
  • You want to spread out the cost or the disruption.
  • Other work, like a kitchen remodel, is coming and can share the wall openings.
  • The failures are few and clearly isolated.

A whole-house repipe fits when

  • Most of the system is the same aging material.
  • Leaks keep appearing in new places.
  • Pressure and water color are poor throughout.
  • You would rather open the walls once and be done.

Living in the house during the work

A repipe usually does not require you to move out, but expect interruptions. The water is shut off for stretches of each work day, and we plan those periods with you so you can cook, shower and use the bathroom beforehand or afterward.

Dust and noise are real, especially when walls are opened. We cover floors, ask you to clear the work areas, and keep pets and children away. Tell us about home offices, medical needs or anyone who needs water at specific times, so we can plan around them.

Clear access

Move furniture, storage and wall hangings away from the areas that will be opened. Closets near bathrooms are often access points.

Plan for water outages

Store drinking water, and arrange for showers and laundry on days when the system is off.

Protect valuables

Remove breakables, electronics and anything fragile from nearby shelves, since cutting and drilling shake walls.

Lead-suspect lines and what to do about them

Older homes may have lead in the service line, in solder, or in old fittings, and a visual check is the first step. A magnet and a scratch test can hint at the pipe's material, but they are not proof. Lab testing and the utility's records are the reliable sources.

If your house has suspect pipe, say so on the first call. Replacing it is part of the plan, and the utility's Lead Service Line resources at mpwc.com can help you learn what is known at your address. Our water line repair page covers the street-to-house section.

Standards and rules behind this advice

Copper tube in plumbing is made to ASTM B88 and PEX tubing to ASTM F876, and both are used with fittings certified to NSF/ANSI 61 for drinking water. Plumbing work in New Jersey falls under the state Uniform Construction Code, which the Pennsauken Construction Code Department enforces locally, and repiping normally needs a permit.
