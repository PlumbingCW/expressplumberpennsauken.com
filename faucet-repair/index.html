Supply valve stuck or burst under the sink

The small valve under a sink, called an angle stop, is the first line of defense when a fixture fails. When it works, you turn it clockwise and the water stops. When it is old, corroded or cracked, it may not budge, or it may leak at the handle the moment you touch it.

If the angle stop will not close

  • Do not force it with pliers, since it can snap off and spray.
  • Go to the main shut-off valve and turn it clockwise.
  • Open a faucet at the lowest level to drain the pipes.
  • Place a bucket and towels below, and photograph the valve.

Call the 24/7 line right away if

  • Water is spraying or pooling and spreading.
  • The main valve will not close either.
  • Water has reached an outlet, a light or the panel.
  • The cabinet floor is soft or the ceiling below is dripping.

Emergency repairs of this kind are common after a tight valve cracks or a braided line bursts at the nut. We answer at night and on weekends, and arrange same-day visits when the schedule allows. Dripping and leaking faucets: what is wearing out

A drip is a small sign of a worn part. Over a month, it can waste a surprising amount of water and leave mineral stains in the sink. The cause depends on the faucet type, so a plumber identifies the style before reaching for parts.

Leaks at the handle or the base usually come from a worn O-ring or cartridge. A leak from the spout points to a worn seat or washer. A faucet that slowly loses pressure may be clogged with mineral buildup inside the aerator or the cartridge.

Kitchen, bathroom and laundry sinks

Kitchen sink

Heavy use wears faucets and hoses. Low flow from a pull-down sprayer is often a clogged aerator or a kinked hose, and leaks under the sink usually come from supply lines or the drain trap.

Bathroom sink

Pop-up drains, supply lines and cartridge faucets cause most calls. A slow drain is often hair on the stopper, not a bigger clog.

Laundry sink

Utility faucets sit on older valves and are often neglected. Replace brittle hoses and check the shut-off valves before they fail.

Outdoor spigots

A hose bib that drips behind the wall can damage the framing. Close it for cold weather and check the inside shut-off.

Shower valve leaks and showerhead problems

A showerhead that drips after you turn it off is usually a worn cartridge or a failed seat in the valve behind the wall. A weak, uneven spray is more often mineral scale in the holes, which soaking in vinegar can loosen. If the handle moves but the temperature will not change, the cartridge or mixing mechanism is worn.

If water stains appear on a ceiling or wall near a shower, that is a different category. Stop using the shower and call us, because a leak inside the wall needs leak detection before repairs, not guesswork.

Bathtub faucet repair

A tub spout that drips, a diverter that sends water to the spout when the shower is running, or a handle that spins without effect all come from a worn tub valve or diverter. Replacing the cartridge often fixes these. In some older homes the valve body is corroded and needs to be replaced, which means opening the wall behind the fixture.

Shower and tub drain clogs

Hair and soap scum are the usual culprits in a slow tub or shower drain. A drain cover, a plunger and a hair catcher handle many cases. When water backs up and does not drain, or other fixtures gurgle, the clog is deeper in the line, and our drain cleaning service is the right next step.

Repair or replace the fixture?

Repair is the right answer when the fixture is sound and a cartridge, seal or washer is worn. Replacement makes more sense when the finish is flaking, the body is corroded or the faucet is no longer made, so parts cannot be found. The cost of the part matters less than the age and condition of the whole fixture.

Check the plumber's license before work starts

New Jersey law says plumbing may be done only by a master plumber, an authorized plumbing contractor, or W-2 employees of one. Advertising and invoices must show the master plumber's name and license number. Ask for the number, then verify it with the State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers, part of the NJ Division of Consumer Affairs.

Before we arrive

These small steps help the visit go quickly.

  • Clear the cabinet under the sink and lay down a towel or tray.
  • Take a photo of the faucet and any visible brand name.
  • Note which handle or spout leaks, and when it began.
  • Check whether the shut-off valves under the sink turn.
  • Know where the main valve is, in case the angle stop fails.

Standards and rules behind this advice

The EPA WaterSense specification limits bathroom faucets to 1.5 gallons per minute, so a WaterSense aerator or faucet is a good replacement standard to look for. Faucets and supply parts that touch drinking water should be certified to NSF/ANSI 61.
