When a business plumbing failure is an emergency

Plumbing trouble in a commercial space stops work faster than in a house. A restroom out of service can force a closure, a food prep sink that will not drain can fail an inspection, and water under a stored pallet can ruin inventory overnight.

Treat it as an emergency when water is spreading, sewage is coming up, or a drain backs up near electrical gear. Shut the closest valve, stop using the affected fixtures and call. Nights, weekends and holidays are covered by the same 24/7 line.

For a single clogged sink or a toilet that will not flush, our drain cleaning team is often the right first call. For larger lines, the work overlaps with sewer line cleaning.

Tenant or landlord: who calls and who pays

Commercial leak detection takes access and time

A hidden leak in a business is harder to find than one in a house. Pipes run above ceilings, under slabs and behind finished walls, and there may be several tenants on one line.

A realistic leak hunt starts with the water meter and a shutoff test to see which zone is leaking. Then comes acoustic listening, pressure tests and, where it helps, thermal imaging to narrow the spot. Each step needs access, and some need the building empty and quiet.

Plan for time. A leak hunt in a large space can take longer than a house, and we will tell you what we find at each stage. For single-family leaks, see our leak detection page.

Floor drains and restroom fixtures

Floor drains in garages, restrooms and mechanical rooms are easy to ignore until they back up. Debris, grease and dried-out traps cause most slow drains and odors, and a drain that backs up when another fixture runs usually points to a shared line.

In restrooms, flush valves wear out from daily use. A valve that sticks open or runs on wastes water, and one that barely flushes needs rebuilding or replacement. Commercial toilets and urinals use different valves than residential ones, so parts are matched to the fixture.

Grease backups and grease trap cleaning

Restaurants, bakeries and prep kitchens send grease into the drain every day. Once it cools in the pipe, it builds up and backs into sinks and floor drains. A grease trap or interceptor only works if it is cleaned on a schedule, and a full one sends the load downstream.

Ask the Pennsauken Sewerage Authority what applies to your business before you change how grease is handled. We can clear the backed-up line, and we can help you plan regular trap service. When a line is coated, hydro jetting often follows.

Backflow testing for commercial accounts

Backflow devices protect the public water supply from contamination. Test rules come from the water utility and code officials, not from the plumber, so ask MPWC or the Construction Code Department what applies to your account.

If a device fails a test, it may need a rebuild or replacement. We can repair a failed assembly and make sure the work follows the permit process.

What to have ready when you call

A few details speed up an after-hours dispatch.

  • The building address and which suite or bay is affected.
  • Where the nearest shutoff valve is, and whether you have already closed it.
  • Whether power or equipment is near the water.
  • The name and phone number of the building manager or landlord.
  • Any lease language on plumbing responsibility.
  • Whether a kitchen, grease trap or backflow device is involved.

Standards and rules behind this advice

Plumbing work in New Jersey falls under the state Uniform Construction Code, which the Pennsauken Construction Code Department enforces locally, and commercial work usually needs permits and inspections. Electrical connections for pumps and equipment follow the National Electrical Code (NEC). Ask the Merchantville Pennsauken Water Commission what its backflow testing guidelines require for your account.
