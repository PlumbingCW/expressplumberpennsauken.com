When a hidden leak is an emergency

Most hidden leaks start small, but a few need action right now. Water pouring through a ceiling, a hissing sound inside a wall, or a floor that feels warm underfoot all point to pressurized water escaping.

Treat it as an emergency if water is reaching light fixtures, outlets or the electric panel, or if the loss is fast enough that a bucket fills in minutes. Shut the main valve, stay out of wet areas near electrical equipment, and call the 24/7 line.

Same-day visits are available when the schedule allows, and after-hours calls get the same triage as daytime ones. If the water is coming from a split pipe, burst pipe repair explains the shut-off in more detail.

  • Shut the main water valve (usually near the meter or where the line enters the basement).
  • Turn off power to a wet area at the breaker only if the panel and your footing are completely dry.
  • Move belongings off the floor and photograph the damage before you clean up.
  • Do not open walls or lift flooring yourself. Opening the wrong spot often makes the real source harder to trace.

Meter still running or bill jumped? Start here

A water bill that suddenly climbs, with no change in how many people live in the house, is one of the most common first clues to a leak. The meter will confirm it faster than guessing. If the dial moves while every fixture is off, water is going somewhere.

Your meter belongs to the utility, not to you. Most Pennsauken addresses are served by the Merchantville Pennsauken Water Commission, and a few by New Jersey American Water, so check your bill to see which one reads yours. Ask that utility any questions about the meter itself or how a billing period was calculated.

Turn everything off

Close all faucets and stop the dishwasher, washer and ice maker. Make sure nobody flushes during the test.

Find the meter and read it

Lift the cover and note the numbers on the register. Take a photo so you can compare later.

Look at the low-flow indicator

Many meters have a small triangle, star or dial that spins when even a trickle passes through. If it turns with everything off, a leak is likely.

Wait and compare

Check again after an hour or two without using water. A changed reading confirms the leak, and a shut main valve that stops the movement points to the house side of the line.

How we locate a leak without opening the wall

No single tool finds every leak. A plumber usually combines several methods, starting with the least invasive and narrowing down until the spot is confirmed.

The table shows what each method is good at, and where it falls short.

Leak location methods and what each can and cannot do
MethodWhat it findsLimit
Acoustic listeningThe sound of water escaping from a pressurized pipe, through floors, walls or soilQuiet leaks and noisy surroundings can mask the signal
Thermal imagingTemperature differences from hot water lines or wet materials behind a surfaceShows a pattern, not always the exact joint
Moisture metersDampness in drywall, wood and masonry, mapped across a wall or ceilingCannot tell a supply leak from condensation by itself
Pressure testingWhether a closed section of pipe holds pressure, which isolates the failing runConfirms a leak exists, then other tools place it
Tracer gasA harmless gas pushed into a drained line that escapes at the hole and is sniffed outNeeds a line that can be isolated and filled
Pipe locatorsThe path and depth of buried metal or plastic lines so digging starts in the right placeOnly maps the route, it does not detect water by itself

Slab leak: signs that water is escaping under the floor

A slab leak is a break in a water line that runs in or beneath a concrete slab floor. Many Pennsauken homes have basements or crawlspaces with piping hung in the open, so slab-on-grade leaks mostly affect slab foundations, additions and some newer construction. If your house sits on a slab, the signs below deserve a prompt call.

A warm spot on the floor

A hot water line leaking under a slab can warm the surface above it. Bare feet notice it before anything else does.

Hissing or running water sound

Listen in a quiet room at night with the fixtures off. A steady trickle or hiss under the floor suggests a pressurized line.

Pressure that drops

Weak flow at several fixtures, with no clog and no change at the street, can mean water is leaving the system before it reaches you.

Damp carpet, lifting tile or musty smell

Moisture that returns after drying, especially along one line of the room, often traces back to a slab line.

Once the area is mapped, the usual options are repairing the line in place, rerouting it through walls or the attic, or replacing the run. Rerouting often costs less disruption than breaking concrete, and we explain both before work starts.

Stain on a ceiling or wall, but no obvious source

Water travels along framing and pipes before it shows itself, so the stain is rarely under the leak. A brown ring on a ceiling can come from a bathroom above, a roof problem, a heating line or a drain joint.

A moisture map and thermal scan usually narrow the source to one wall cavity. Tell us when the stain appeared and whether it grows after showers, rain or heating cycles, because that timing separates a plumbing leak from a roof or condensation problem.

Basement and crawlspace leaks

Pennsauken's median house was built in 1964, and homes of that era often have supply lines, drain stacks and shut-off valves exposed under the first floor. That helps, because a leak there can often be seen, heard and traced without breaking anything open.

We check joints, valves, the water heater connections and the meter setup first. Look for drips on pipe bottoms, green or white crust on copper, rust bloom on older galvanized steel, and damp insulation. Water on a basement floor can also come from outside or from a sump problem, so we sort supply leaks from drainage issues before recommending a fix.

Buried line leaks between the street and the house

The service line runs underground from the utility's connection to your meter or foundation. A leak there can run for months without surfacing, which shows up as a rising bill, a soft or green patch of lawn, or pressure that sags.

We confirm the leak with a pressure check and listening, then use a pipe locator to trace the path so digging is limited to one spot. Ask MPWC where their responsibility ends and yours begins at your address. The details of repairing or replacing the line are on our water line repair page.

Pennsauken water: who to call and what to check

Most Pennsauken addresses are on the Merchantville Pennsauken Water Commission, whose office is at 6751 Westfield Ave and whose water emergencies line is (856) 663-0044. Their staff can tell you about the meter, usage history and anything on the street side.

If the problem is on your side of the connection, you call us. If a main on the street is leaking or a meter is damaged, the utility handles it. A quick call to sort out which side the leak is on avoids paying a plumber to dig up something the utility owns.

Smaller cases: sprinklers, pools and the meter box

Irrigation leaks hide because the zones run on a timer. A damp patch, a lawn that stays soggy after the cycle ends, or a pressure drop when the system starts all suggest a cracked riser or fitting. Isolating each zone with the valves usually shows which one is losing water.

Pool and spa leaks are harder to judge, since evaporation also drops the level. A bucket test and a pressure check of the plumbing lines tell the two apart. Water pooling around the meter box in the yard is a different case: the box and meter belong to the utility, so report that one to MPWC first.

Standards and rules behind this advice

The EPA WaterSense program publishes a simple meter-based leak check: read the meter, avoid using any water for a set period, then read it again.
