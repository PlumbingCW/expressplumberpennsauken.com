Bathroom Faucet Repair in Pennsauken, NJ: Why It Drips and What Fixes It

A dripping bathroom faucet almost always comes down to one worn part inside the handle. Compression faucets with two handles usually need a new washer or seat, single-handle faucets usually need a new cartridge or O-ring, and weak or sideways spray usually points to a clogged aerator. Close the small stop valve under the sink, swap the worn part, and the drip stops.

This guide for Pennsauken, NJ homeowners shows how to tell which part failed and which repairs are fair to try yourself. It also explains when a stuck shutoff valve or corroded fixture means a licensed plumber in Camden County should take over.

Start with the handle: which kind of faucet do you have?

The repair depends on how the faucet is built, so look at the handles first. Two separate handles that turn several times before the water stops are compression faucets. One lever or knob that moves up, down, and side to side is almost always a cartridge or ball style.

Homes in Pennsauken have a median build year of 1964, so homes of that era often still have a mix of original and replaced fixtures. A bathroom may hold a decades-old two-handle set next to a newer single lever. Treat each faucet as its own repair.

Washers and O-rings are small rubber parts that press against metal every time you shut the water off. Over time they harden, crack, or flatten, and water slips past. A cartridge is a sealed unit that does the same job in a single-handle faucet, which is why replacing it often cures several symptoms at once.

Cartridge, washer, or aerator: how to tell them apart

Washer and seat problems

A drip that starts after you close the handle firmly points to the washer. Pitted metal under the washer chews up a new washer quickly, so a repeat drip means the seat needs attention too.

Cartridge problems

A single-handle faucet that drips, runs hot when set to cold, or feels loose is usually a worn cartridge. The replacement must match the brand and model, so photograph the faucet and keep the old cartridge for comparison.

Aerator problems

The aerator is the small screen at the tip of the spout. When grit or mineral flakes block it, flow weakens or the stream splits, but the faucet does not usually drip because of it.

Shutoff valves: the step that decides how the repair goes

Every bathroom sink should have two small stop valves under it, one for hot and one for cold. Turn each clockwise until it stops, then open the faucet to confirm the water has really quit. If water keeps running, the stop valve is not sealing.

Valves that have not been turned in many years can seize or break when forced. Do not keep cranking. Shut the main water valve for the house instead and have the stop valves replaced as part of the job.

Worth knowing about a slow drip

The U.S. EPA reports that a faucet dripping once per second can waste more than 3,000 gallons a year. A drip also stains the basin over time.

A careful DIY repair, step by step

Close the stop valves and drain the line

Turn off both stop valves, then open the faucet to release pressure.

Remove the handle and trim

Pry off the cap, remove the screw, and lift the handle.

Pull the worn part and match it

Take the washer, O-ring, or cartridge to a hardware store, or look up the faucet model. Parts that are close but not exact often leak again.

Reassemble and test slowly

Open the stop valves partway, then fully, and check for drips at the handle and under the sink.

Reasonable to do yourself

  • Cleaning or replacing an aerator
  • Swapping a washer or O-ring on a faucet with working stop valves
  • Replacing a cartridge when you can identify the exact model
  • Tightening a loose handle or packing nut

When to call a plumber

  • A stop valve will not close or leaks when touched
  • Corroded or crumbling supply lines under the sink
  • Water staining or soft cabinet floors below the sink
  • The fixture is old enough that parts are no longer made
  • You want the faucet replaced rather than repaired

When a repair is not worth it

A cartridge or washer swap is a small job on a sound faucet. It makes less sense on a faucet with a worn finish, a cracked body, or parts that cannot be found. A plumber can compare the repair against installing a new faucet and explain the cost drivers before any work begins.

If repeated fixes keep failing, a plumber doing faucet repair can compare the options on site. If the sink also drains slowly, the trap may need drain cleaning while the cabinet is open. Plumbers who serve Collingswood and Cherry Hill handle these same repairs.

What Pennsauken homeowners should know about water and faucets

Most Pennsauken addresses are supplied by the Merchantville Pennsauken Water Commission, and a few may be on New Jersey American Water, so check your bill. MPWC draws from wells in the Potomac-Raritan-Magothy aquifer and publishes an annual water quality report on its website.

The reports are where to look for hardness. Mineral scale is a common reason aerators clog, so if you see white flakes in the screen, ask MPWC for the hardness in its report or test your tap before choosing a faucet finish. If you suspect a problem on the street side of your meter, MPWC lists a 24-hour water emergency number on its website.

EPA also suggests a simple leak test: read the water meter, use no water for two hours, then read it again. If the reading moved, something is leaking.
