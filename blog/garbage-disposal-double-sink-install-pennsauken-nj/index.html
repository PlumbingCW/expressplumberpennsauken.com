Installing a Garbage Disposal in a Double Sink in Pennsauken, NJ

A disposal fits a double sink by mounting to the drain of one basin, with the second basin joined to the disposal outlet through a tee and then one shared trap. The job has four parts: mount the unit, make the electrical connection, connect the drains, and test for leaks. The manufacturer's manual for your exact model governs every step.

A careful homeowner can handle the mounting and drain hookup on a simple setup. The parts that bring in a professional are altered drain pipes, a missing outlet or switch, and anything involving a permit. Our Pennsauken, NJ plumbers in Camden County can take over at whatever step you stop.

How a disposal fits a double sink

In a double sink, each basin has its own drain opening. The disposal replaces the strainer on one side, usually the basin with the larger bowl or the side nearer the dishwasher. The other basin sends its water through a tailpiece and a continuous waste tee into the disposal outlet.

From there everything leaves through a single trap and into the wall. The result is two sinks sharing one trap, which is why the pipe arrangement matters more than the disposal itself.

What you need to line up before you start

Before you buy or cut anything

A short survey of the cabinet avoids most wrong turns.

  • Your model's owner manual, since the mounting system and hose fittings differ.
  • Room under the sink for the disposal, a trap and the existing garbage can or storage.
  • A dishwasher connection point. Check whether the dishwasher drains into the sink drain now.
  • A switched outlet or a switch location for the disposal, and whether an outlet exists under the sink.
  • The existing tailpiece, tee and trap condition. Corroded or cracked pieces should be replaced first.
  • A plan for turning off the water to the sink and clearing the cabinet.

Drain and trap configuration: where installs go wrong

The usual double sink layout puts the disposal on one side, a tailpiece on the other and a tee between them leading to the trap. The disposal outlet points sideways into that tee. If the old pipes sit too low or too far apart, the geometry no longer works without new pieces.

The most common failure is a trap arrangement that holds water in the wrong place or creates a slow drain. A disposal also needs a clear route for its outlet to reach the tee. If adjustments mean cutting into the wall pipe, treat it as a plumbing installation job instead of a weekend fix.

The dishwasher knockout

Many disposals ship with a small plug on the dishwasher inlet. KitchenAid's installation guidance describes knocking the plug out with a hammer and screwdriver, driving it into the disposal, then pulling it out with needle-nose pliers. If you skip this step, the dishwasher cannot drain into the unit.

Retrieve the plug from inside the grinding chamber, because leaving it there can jam the unit. Attach the dishwasher drain hose to the inlet with the clamp supplied, and follow the dishwasher manual for the hose loop or air gap that keeps drain water from flowing back.

Electrical: where DIY ends

InSinkerator's installation page lists the electrical connection as step two, and offers cord or hardwire options on its EZ Connect models. A cord needs a switched outlet under the sink. A hardwired unit connects to a circuit, usually through a wall switch.

Water and electricity share a small cabinet, so this is where caution pays. If the sink has no outlet or switch, or if you are unsure the circuit can carry the load, have a licensed electrician do the wiring. A plumber handles the pipes and mounting around it.

A homeowner can usually handle

  • Removing the old strainer and mounting the new unit per the manual.
  • Knocking out the dishwasher plug and clamping the hose.
  • Connecting the tailpiece and trap when the existing pipes line up.
  • Running water and checking every joint for drips.

Call a licensed pro for

  • Cutting or moving drain pipe, or rebuilding the trap.
  • New wiring, a switch or a new outlet, which needs an electrician.
  • Persistent leaks after a retry, or a sink that drains slowly after the install.
  • Work a permit may cover. See the garbage disposal service for help.

Permits for a disposal install in Pennsauken

Pennsauken permits for plumbing work are handled by the township Construction Code Department at 2400 Merchantville Ave, (856) 665-1000, extension 1401, with a plumbing subcode official at extension 1406. Township Code Chapter 133 requires permits for construction except ordinary repairs, and plumbing permit fees are set in a schedule. Fees can change, so ask the department for the current schedule.

Whether a new disposal with new wiring needs a permit is a question for the department, not for this guide. Call before you start, and ask about both the plumbing and any electrical work. New Jersey allows plumbing work only by a master plumber, an authorized plumbing contractor or their employees. Ask any hired plumber for a license number and verify it with the State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers.

Test before you close the cabinet

Check the joints dry

Run a hand along every slip nut and flange, then dry them before water goes in.

Run water first

Fill both basins and let them drain with the disposal off. Watch the tee, trap and dishwasher connection for drips.

Power up with water running

Turn the disposal on with a steady stream of cold water. Listen for scraping or humming and shut it off at once if you hear either.

Run the dishwasher

Start a drain cycle and watch the hose joint. A leak here means a loose clamp or a missed knockout.

Standards and rules behind this advice

Manufacturer installation instructions set the order of drain, dishwasher knockout and mounting, and the National Electrical Code (NEC) covers the disposal circuit.
