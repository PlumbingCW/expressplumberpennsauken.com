Residential Sewer Pipe Lining in Pennsauken, NJ: How It Works

Sewer pipe lining repairs the underground line between your house and the street from the inside, so a long trench is often avoided. A plumber cameras the pipe, cleans it, pulls in a resin-soaked liner, cures it into a new pipe within the old one, and then reopens any connections. It works when the old pipe is still roughly round and in place.

It does not work on every line. A collapsed section, a bad offset or a belly full of standing water usually needs a different repair. Our sewer line repair team in Pennsauken, NJ starts with a camera, so the choice is based on what the pipe really looks like.

The lining process, step by step

Camera inspection

A camera runs through the lateral from a cleanout or fixture, showing cracks, roots, offsets and low spots.

Cleaning the pipe

Roots, scale and debris are cut or jetted out, because a liner will not bond to a dirty wall.

Liner preparation

A flexible tube soaked in resin is sized to the pipe and prepared for insertion.

Insertion and cure

The liner is pulled or inverted into place, pressed to the wall and cured with heat, light or time.

Reinstating and re-camera

Connections are reopened, then a second camera pass confirms the new pipe is smooth and open.

The steps are the same idea whether the job is short or long, but cleaning is where many jobs are won or lost. A thin liner on top of heavy scale leaves wrinkles that catch paper later.

This post is about the underground lateral to the street, not the vertical cast iron stack inside your walls. Those are separate repairs with different tools.

Who owns what: house, lateral and street

The part of the sewer inside your property line is typically the homeowner's, while the public main under the street belongs to the system. Exactly where one stops and the other starts depends on local rules and records, so ask the Pennsauken Sewerage Authority before you assume.

In Pennsauken, the Authority collects sewage and the Camden County Municipal Utilities Authority treats it. Knowing which side a break falls on decides who pays and who repairs, so get an answer in writing if you can.

Pennsauken permits and approvals for sewer work

The township code lists the Pennsauken Sewerage Authority among the approvals that permits can depend on. Plumbing permits are issued by the Construction Code Department, 2400 Merchantville Ave, 856-665-1000.

What this means for you: before a sewer lining job, ask the Construction Code Department whether your specific repair counts as ordinary repair, and ask the Authority about any connection approval. We do not decide that for you from memory. CCMUA also closed a large loan to separate combined and stormwater sewers in Pennsauken, but that program does not change who owns your lateral.

Signs the lateral may be failing

Backups that return

A line cleared by a plumber that clogs again within weeks.

Gurgling in several drains

Air pushed back through traps as water struggles to move.

Wet or sunken spots outside

A lawn patch that stays soggy or sinks over the line path.

Root intrusion history

Mature trees near the line often send roots into joints.

Homes in Pennsauken with a median build year near 1964 often have laterals from that era, with older materials and joints that can separate over decades. That is a general pattern, not a claim about your house, which is why a camera inspection comes first.

Before the lining crew arrives

A little preparation keeps the day on track.

  • Find your cleanout and clear access to it
  • Limit water use during the work window
  • Move vehicles from the area near the cleanout or driveway
  • Ask for the camera footage before and after
  • Ask who handles the permit and inspection
  • If sewage is on the floor, avoid contact and keep children and pets away

Standards and rules behind this advice

Cured-in-place lining is commonly specified to ASTM F1216 or ASTM F1743, and the New Jersey Uniform Construction Code governs permits.
