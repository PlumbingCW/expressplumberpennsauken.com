Sump Pump Battery Backup in Pennsauken, NJ: A Homeowner Guide

A sump pump battery backup is a second pump in the same pit that runs on a battery when the power goes out or the main pump fails. It has its own float switch, a charger, and usually an alarm. When water rises and the main pump cannot respond, the backup starts pumping so the basement stays dry for as long as the battery holds.

This guide for Pennsauken, NJ homeowners explains how backups work, how battery systems compare with water-powered ones, what to test and when, and what an installation involves. Storms are the usual reason the power fails exactly when the pit fills.

How a battery backup sump pump works

The main pump runs on household power and sits at the bottom of the pit. A second pump, mounted a little higher, waits on its own float switch. If the water climbs past the main pump's on level, because the main pump failed or the power is out, the backup turns on.

The battery system includes a deep-cycle battery, a charger that keeps it topped up, and often an alarm. Ready.gov, the federal preparedness site, suggests considering a sump pump with a battery, which is the idea behind this setup.

Battery or water-powered: which backup fits your basement?

Water useNoneUses household water while it runs
MaintenanceBattery age, charger, terminalsCheck valve, venturi, supply line

Trade-offBattery wears out and must be replacedWater use, and typically less flow than a battery pump

Battery systems are the common choice because they pump more water and are easier to understand. Water-powered units remove the battery, but they add to your water use while they run, and they depend on the supply staying under pressure.

If you are unsure which suits your basement, a plumber doing sump pump installation can size the backup to the pump, the pit, and how fast water has entered in past storms.

What installation involves

Check the pit and the main pump

The pit needs enough room for a second pump and its float. A pump near the end of its life may be better replaced at the same visit.

Set the backup float height

The backup float sits slightly above the main pump's float so it only starts when needed. Both must move freely without touching the pit wall.

Run a separate discharge or tie in correctly

Each pump needs its own check valve to stop water falling back into the pit. A shared discharge needs a proper fitting.

Mount the battery and controller

The battery goes on a stable shelf or stand above any water, with ventilation as the manual directs. The charger plugs into a working outlet.

Test it with water

The plumber fills the pit and watches both pumps start. The alarm and the float height are checked before leaving.

Maintenance that keeps the backup working

Seasonal sump backup checks

Do these a few times a year and before forecast storms.

  • Pour water into the pit until the main pump starts, then confirm it shuts off
  • Lift the backup float or unplug the main pump to confirm the backup starts
  • Check the battery terminals for corrosion and the charger light
  • Read the battery date and plan replacement before it wears out
  • Clear the discharge line outside and confirm water flows away from the house
  • Press the alarm test button, if the unit has one

Worth knowing about generators

EPA and Ready.gov both warn that gasoline generators and similar machines must run only outdoors and away from windows, doors, and vents, because the exhaust contains carbon monoxide. If you power a sump pump from a generator, never run it inside a basement, garage, or shed.

What a backup cannot do

A backup pump can fail too, and a large storm can bring in more water than a small pump can move. A backup does nothing for water coming up a floor drain from the sewer, which needs a backwater valve and a different fix.

If water keeps entering the basement even when the pumps run, the problem is the water path into the foundation. That is where basement waterproofing comes in: exterior drainage, interior drain tile, or crack repair can lower how hard the pump has to work.

What Pennsauken homeowners should know about sewers and flooding

In 2024 the Camden County Municipal Utilities Authority closed a NJ Water Bank loan for work in Pennsauken. The money pays to split combined stormwater and sanitary sewers and to separate water and sewer mains. The stated aims are better water quality and less localized flooding, and the work is part of CCMUA's combined sewer overflow long-term control plan.

That is a utility project, and no schedule or household effect has been published for individual homes. So treat it as background, not a substitute for protection inside your own basement. A sump pump and a backup handle groundwater, and the sewer project does not change that need.

For sewer backup questions, the Pennsauken Sewerage Authority collects local sewage and CCMUA treats it. Ready.gov also advises installing check valves, which is a separate fix from a sump pump.
