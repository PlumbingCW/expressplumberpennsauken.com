Boiler Repair in Pennsauken, NJ

When a boiler stops sending heat to the radiators, the cause is often small: a thermostat setting, low pressure or air in the lines. We diagnose and repair hot water and steam boilers in older homes across Pennsauken, NJ.

No heat: what to check before calling

Check the thermostat

Confirm it is set to heat, set above room temperature and has fresh batteries if it needs them.

Look at the boiler pressure gauge

Compare the reading with the range in your boiler manual. A reading near zero suggests a leak or low fill, and one that is far above range means call a plumber.

Check power and the switch

Many boilers have a service switch that looks like a light switch near the stairs. Make sure it is on and the breaker has not tripped.

Feel the radiators

If the boiler is hot but upper radiators stay cool, air may be trapped. If the boiler itself is cold, the problem is on the boiler side.

Stop and call if anything looks wrong

Water on the floor, a burning smell, soot or a boiler that keeps shutting off all need a licensed plumber.

Hot water or steam: why it matters

Older homes in Pennsauken may have either type, and the two behave differently. Check the boiler label and the radiators. A steam system has a sight glass and vents on each radiator, while a hot water system has a pressure gauge and an expansion tank. We also serve older heating systems in Maple Shade.

Bleeding radiators the safe way

You can do

  • Turn the thermostat down and let the system cool
  • Place a cloth under the bleeder valve
  • Open the valve a quarter turn with a radiator key until water spits steadily
  • Close it and check the boiler pressure afterward
  • Repeat on each upper radiator

Call a plumber when

  • The boiler pressure keeps dropping after you add water
  • Radiators stay cold with the pump running
  • Water leaks from the bleeder or a pipe joint
  • You have a steam system, which should not be bled
  • The boiler shuts off by itself

A leaking relief valve or wet floor

The pressure relief valve is a safety device. If it drips, pressure is too high, the expansion tank has failed or the valve itself is worn. Do not cap or plug it.

Turn off the boiler at its power switch, let it cool and call. If the water is hot or steaming, keep away. A leak at a pipe joint or the boiler jacket often means corrosion and needs inspection.

Banging, kettling and gurgling

Banging

Pipes pitched wrong or water trapped in a steam line often cause banging. Loose pipe straps can also knock.

Kettling

A rumbling like boiling in a kettle suggests scale or sludge on the heat exchanger. It needs a flush or cleaning, and ignoring it can shorten the boiler's life.

Gurgling

Air in hot water lines produces gurgling. Bleeding radiators may help, but constant air points to a leak or a failed air eliminator.

Clicking at start

Pipes expand as they heat. Light ticking is normal. Sharp cracks need a look.

Gas smell and carbon monoxide

A gas smell means leave right away. Skip the switches and phones inside, then call the gas utility or 911 from the yard or street.

Carbon monoxide has no odor. If a detector sounds, or anyone has headaches, nausea or dizziness, move everyone into fresh air and call 911. Each level of a home with a fuel-burning boiler should have a working CO detector.

Annual service, repair or replace

A yearly service by a licensed plumber checks combustion, venting, controls and water level before the heating season. It catches small faults when they are cheap. Older cast iron boilers can last a long time, but parts and efficiency eventually fall behind.

Standards and rules behind this advice

Heating boilers are built to the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, and gas piping follows NFPA 54, the National Fuel Gas Code. Carbon monoxide alarms are listed to the UL 2034 standard. According to the manufacturer's manual, relief valve and pressure settings are not for guessing.

What to do if you smell gas or suspect carbon monoxide

Treat both as a safety event first and a repair second.

  • Leave the house with everyone and pets, and do not switch lights or appliances on or off.
  • Call 911 or the gas utility from outside.
  • Do not go back in until responders say it is safe.
  • Call the 24/7 line afterward for the repair.
