Choosing between tank, tankless and hybrid before you buy

The unit you pick sets the scope of the whole job. A like-for-like tank is the simplest install. A tankless or heat pump hybrid unit changes the gas, venting, electrical and drain work around it.

Venting: the part older houses surprise people on

A gas heater must exhaust its combustion gases safely. In homes of the 1960s and earlier, that often means an old masonry chimney that was sized for a larger, older appliance. A modern, more efficient unit can cool the flue gases too much, which can cause condensation and poor draft.

Options include a chimney liner, a power-vent unit that exits through a side wall, or a sealed-combustion model. A plumber checks the flue before quoting, and the result decides which unit you can buy.

Gas line, electric circuit and the other utilities

Gas units need a correctly sized line, a shutoff and a drip leg. Electric units need a circuit with the right breaker and wire size for the element load. Moving from one fuel to the other usually means new work on both sides.

Gas supply

Pipe size and run length decide whether the existing line can feed a bigger or tankless unit.

Electric circuit

Hybrid and electric models call for a dedicated circuit. Panel capacity is checked before anything is ordered.

Condensate drain

High-efficiency gas and hybrid models produce water that needs a safe place to drain.

Relief valve line

The discharge pipe must run to a safe point and never be capped.

Space, combustion air and clearances

A gas heater needs air to burn, and a closet or finished room can starve it. The plumber looks at the size of the space, the louvers or openings and the clearance to walls and shelving.

Hybrid units need open air for a different reason, since they cool the room as they work. A packed utility closet is a poor home for either, and sometimes the better answer is a new spot.

Expansion control and pressure on the supply side

Heating water makes it expand. In a plumbing system with a check valve or backflow device on the supply, that extra volume has nowhere to go and pressure spikes. An expansion tank absorbs it.

Whether yours needs one depends on how the house is plumbed, so the plumber evaluates it on site rather than assuming. If your relief valve has ever dripped on its own, mention that when you call.

Relocating a heater or changing fuel

Moving a heater to a garage, closet or new utility room means extending hot and cold lines, vent routes and drains. It also raises the odds that the job needs a permit, and it can be a good time to update old shutoff valves. Changing fuel type follows the same logic.

Permits and inspection for a Pennsauken, NJ install

The township Construction Code Department, at 2400 Merchantville Ave, handles permits for plumbing work. Township code lists approvals that can involve the Camden County health department, the Pennsauken Sewerage Authority and your water provider, depending on the work.

The practical point: a relocation or fuel change is more likely to involve those approvals than a simple swap. Ask early, and schedule around the inspection, not just the install day.

New construction and remodels

In a remodel or addition, the heater location is decided on paper before walls close. Rough-in goes in early, with a pan, drain, vent path and circuit planned together. Standards and rules behind this advice

Gas storage heaters are built to ANSI Z21.10.1, and Department of Energy guidelines for heat pump models call for a room of adequate volume and air temperature between 40 and 90 degrees Fahrenheit. Plumbing work in New Jersey falls under the state Uniform Construction Code, which the Pennsauken Construction Code Department enforces locally.
